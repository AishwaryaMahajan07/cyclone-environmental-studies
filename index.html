01

Why study cyclones?

Cyclones are atmospheric systems, but their consequences extend into water, soil, biodiversity, infrastructure, health and livelihoods.

ABSTRACT

A tropical cyclone is a rotating low-pressure weather system that develops over sufficiently warm tropical or subtropical oceans. Its hazards are not limited to strong winds: coastal flooding from storm surge, intense rainfall, river flooding and erosion can create cascading environmental and social impacts. This study connects the physical science of cyclones with environmental consequences and disaster-management practices, with India and the 1999 Odisha Super Cyclone as the principal case study.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
  1. Understand how tropical cyclones develop and intensify.
  2. Recognise the main hazards produced by a cyclone.
  3. Explain environmental and socio-economic impacts.
  4. Identify why India's eastern and western coasts face different risks.
  5. Understand how early warning, evacuation, ecosystems and resilient infrastructure reduce losses.
Key ideaHazard becomes disaster when exposure and vulnerability are high.Risk ≈ Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability

The science behind a cyclone

Think of a cyclone as a heat engine: warm ocean water supplies energy, while pressure differences organise air into a rotating circulation.

EYEcalmer core
EYewall
Warm, moist air rises
Heavy rain bands
Air flows outward aloft
1
Warm ocean

Sea-surface temperatures around 26.5°C or higher over a sufficiently deep layer can provide the heat and moisture needed for development.

2
Evaporation & rising air

Warm, moist air rises. Water vapour condenses into clouds and releases latent heat, helping the air column warm further.

3
Lower pressure

As air rises and pressure falls near the centre, surrounding air moves inward, bringing more moisture.

4
Rotation

The Coriolis effect gives the circulation its organised spin. Strong vertical wind shear can disrupt the developing system.

5
Intensification

When ocean heat, moisture, atmospheric structure and outflow remain favourable, the storm can strengthen.

Why coastal India is vulnerable

India has long coastlines bordering the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal. Population, infrastructure, agriculture and ecosystems are concentrated in many coastal and low-lying areas.

ARABIAN SEABAY OF BENGAL
INDIA
Bay of Bengal

Frequently affected by severe tropical cyclones; shallow coastal waters and coastal geometry can increase storm-surge risk in some areas.

Arabian Sea

Also produces cyclones. Recent decades have highlighted the importance of monitoring changing storm behaviour in this basin.

Exposure matters

Dense settlements, ports, roads, power systems, farms and fisheries can turn a natural hazard into a major socio-economic event.

05

Impacts: environment → society → economy

A cyclone rarely creates only one type of damage. Effects can cascade from the physical environment into health, livelihoods and essential services.

01 · ENVIRONMENT

When ecosystems absorb the shock

  • Coastal erosion and sediment redistribution
  • Saltwater intrusion into soils and freshwater
  • Damage to mangroves, wetlands and coastal vegetation
  • Loss or displacement of wildlife and aquatic habitats
02 · SOCIETY

When daily life is disrupted

  • Evacuation and temporary displacement
  • Interrupted electricity, transport and communication
  • Pressure on healthcare, water and sanitation systems
  • Greater risk for children, older people and vulnerable households
03 · ECONOMY

When losses continue after landfall

  • Damage to homes, farms, roads and power networks
  • Loss of crops, livestock and fishing activity
  • Business interruption and supply-chain delays
  • High costs of reconstruction and recovery

Case study: Odisha Super Cyclone, 1999

The 29 October 1999 event shows why cyclone risk must be studied as a combination of atmospheric hazard, coastal exposure and preparedness.

HISTORICAL EVENT · 29 OCTOBER 1999

Odisha's coastline and the Bay of Bengal

The very severe system intensified into a super cyclonic storm before making landfall near Paradip. The India Meteorological Department's synopsis describes winds around 260–270 km/h and a sea-level rise of more than 20 feet in the affected area, with catastrophic flooding and approximately 10,000 deaths.

Primary scientific reference: India Meteorological Department (IMD), Orissa Super Cyclone – A Synopsis.

What does this case teach?

01

Warning must reach people. Scientific forecasts only reduce losses when information becomes timely action.

02

Evacuation capacity matters. Shelters, transport, local coordination and public trust are part of preparedness.

03

Environmental buffers matter. Mangroves, dunes, wetlands and healthy coastal ecosystems can contribute to resilience, although they cannot stop a major cyclone by themselves.

04

Recovery is long-term. Rebuilding homes, livelihoods, infrastructure and ecosystems can take years.

07

Climate change: what should we say carefully?

Climate science is nuanced. A professional environmental-studies report should distinguish observed changes, physical mechanisms and remaining uncertainties.

OCEAN

Warmer seawater can increase available energy

Higher ocean temperatures can provide more heat and moisture to storms when other atmospheric conditions are favourable.

RAIN

A warmer atmosphere can hold more water vapour

This can increase the potential for very heavy rainfall, although the rainfall response varies by storm and region.

SEA LEVEL

Higher baseline sea level raises coastal flood risk

Even without changing storm intensity, a higher starting sea level can make coastal flooding more damaging.

UNCERTAINTY

Not every cyclone trend is simple

Frequency, intensity, track and rainfall can change differently. Regional observations and scientific studies are therefore important.

09

Conclusion

Cyclones are natural atmospheric phenomena, but their consequences are shaped by where people live, how ecosystems are managed, how strong infrastructure is, and how quickly warnings become action. Studying cyclones through both environmental science and disaster management gives a clearer understanding of how societies can reduce risk while protecting ecosystems and livelihoods.
